Case 1 · Reports 01 Performance and 03 Economics

Semi-synthetic
artemisinin.

Artemisinin is the core of the main malaria treatment and normally comes from a plant, sweet wormwood. Between 2004 and 2013 a Berkeley lab, the company Amyris and the drug maker Sanofi taught yeast to make its precursor. The science worked. The factory stopped two years later.

The story in four numbers

Each number belongs to a different report. Together they explain the outcome. Any one of them alone would mislead.

250×

Rise in yeast titer of artemisinic acid, from 0.1 g/L (2006) to 25 g/L (2013).

Report 01 · Ro 2006, Paddon 2013
40–55%

Overall yield of the chemistry that turns the yeast product into the drug. Bench route 40%, Sanofi route about 55%.

Report 01 · Kung 2018
60 t/yr

Design capacity of Sanofi's plant in Garessio, Italy. About a third of world demand.

Report 01 · PATH 2014, Peplow 2016
$350–400/kg

Sanofi's no-profit, no-loss price. Plant artemisinin sold below $250/kg in 2014–15.

Report 03 · Peplow 2016

The 25 g/L result came from 2 L fermenters. Sanofi then spent 2008 to 2013 turning the chemistry into a factory process. Actual tonnes produced per year were never published; only plans and capacity were.

How to write it

WeakThe engineered yeast achieves industrial-level production of artemisinic acid, enabling a stable supply of artemisinin.

"Industrial-level" has no number. "Achieves" hides the scale. The sentence skips the chemistry step, where up to 60% of the material is lost.

StrongIn 2 L fed-batch fermenters the 2013 strain reached 25 g/L artemisinic acid, up from 1.6 g/L in earlier attempts (Paddon et al. 2013). Turning the acid into artemisinin takes a chemical step with 40% overall yield at bench scale and about 55% in Sanofi's plant (Kung et al. 2018), so the fermentation titer overstates what reaches the drug.

Scale, number, unit, source, and the next loss in the chain, all in one paragraph.

Report 03: the price it had to beat

Sanofi's break-even was about $350–400/kg. The plant crop's price moves with the harvest, so the comparison is not against one number but against a range that swung sixfold in a decade.

What happened

  1. 2004 · Plant price jumps from $350 to over $1,000/kg. The Gates Foundation funds the project with $42.6 M.
  2. 2005–07 · Price peaks at $1,100/kg, then falls to $180–250/kg as farmers plant more.
  3. 2013 · 25 g/L published. Garessio plant launches in April; WHO approves the ingredient in May.
  4. 2014 · First 1.7 million treatments ship to six African countries. The plant price falls under $250/kg.
  5. 2015 · Sanofi makes no semi-synthetic artemisinin at all. Lifetime output: over 39 million treatments.
  6. 2016 · Sanofi sells the plant to Huvepharma, which says it will switch to plant material if the yeast route cannot match the price.

How to write it

WeakSemi-synthetic artemisinin is cost-competitive with the plant-derived product.

True in 2011, false in 2015. A single comparison point hides the risk that ended the project.

StrongSanofi set its no-profit, no-loss price at about $350–400/kg (Peplow 2016). Plant artemisinin sold for $1,100/kg in 2005, $530/kg in 2011 and under $250/kg in 2014–15. The yeast route won at two of those three prices and lost at the one that held after the plant opened.

Break-even, the competitor's full range, and which side of it the product sat on.

What ReLeaf takes from this

Report every step's loss

Sanofi's buyers judged 25 g/L multiplied by 40–55%, not 25 g/L. For us: lumen signal, then what crosses the membrane, then what the shell sample shows. Each step gets its own number or the words "not measured".

Name the scale

"2 L fed-batch" is in every strong sentence above. Our equivalent is the loop volume, operating mode (A, B or C) and cross-flow setting for each run.

Compare against a range

When the BOM exists, compare cost per run against the full range of what a farmer pays for the incumbent, not one price. Until then, the claim ledger says "not yet established".
